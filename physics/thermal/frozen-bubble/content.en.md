## What actually freezes? {#step0 h2=s0title pre=1}

::: html step0-1
:::

A soap bubble is like a thin bag holding air. Its wall is a **water-containing liquid film**. When it cools, water in the film can become ice, leaving visible crystal patterns; the air inside stays a gas. Focus on one patch of film first: how does a small piece of ice grow? {#s0body .explain}

① Add a small ice seed → ② Play and watch branches grow from it → ③ Pause, then compare a colder film. The disk shows the film facing you. In this first experiment, crystals stay in place. {#s0task .hint i18n=text}

::: html step0-2
growthAlt: Film facing the viewer: orange dots mark ice starting points and pale branches grow outward. This is not snow inside the bubble.
projection: Front view of the film: orange dots mark ice starting points; pale branches mark new ice. Click the film or use the button to add a seed.
seed: Add an ice seed
play: Play
reset: Reset & stop
timeLabel: Diagram time (arbitrary units; seeking pauses playback)
colderQuestion: What if the film is colder?
colderBody: Once ice exists, compare its growth. How far the film temperature lies below this solution’s melting point is called “undercooling.” At the same diagram time, increase the value below and watch the branch length.
underLabel: How far below melting point? (undercooling ΔT)
underNote: The melting point Tm is the reference temperature for equilibrium between ice and liquid; it depends on the recipe. ΔT = Tm − film temperature. For example, −6.5°C minus −18°C gives 11.5°C. The slider preserves seeds and time to redraw a hypothetical comparison; it does not cool a bubble over time.
:::

The seed you added stands in for the first small, stable patch of ice forming naturally: nucleation. Water nearby then freezes and the crystal grows. Different growth rates in different directions can produce branches. The six-arm drawing helps you recognize growth; it does not predict a real crystal’s shape. {#growthWhy .explain i18n=text}

## How can ice crystals travel over the film? {#step1 h2=s1title pre=1}

::: html step1-1
:::

You have seen growth; now bring the whole bubble back into view. Compare two environments: A develops moving crystals, while B mainly freezes upward from its base. **Observe the difference first, then ask what carries the ice.** {#s1body .explain}

::: html step1-2
researchTitle: Which study is this comparison based on?
researchConditions: Ahmadi and colleagues used approximately 79% water, 20% glycerol and 1% dish soap by volume, with a melting point of about −6.5°C. A: air and icy base near −18°C. B: air near +25°C and bases from −10 to −40°C; this diagram selects −20°C as an example. Both began freezing on contact with an icy base.
researchScope: See Experimental setup, Figs. 2–4 and Methods in the original paper. These are redrawn qualitative diagrams, not its measured times, paths or front heights. Recipe, size and surroundings matter.
researchBridge: Use this source card as the map for the labels below: A = all cold; B = warm air + a cold base. The controls hold both cases at one diagram time. First compare the visible crystal paths and freezing front; then return to the explanation of heat and liquid motion below.
:::

After watching motion, ask what drives it {#mechanismTitle .mechanism-title tag=h3 i18n=text}

::: html step1-4
cause1: Freezing releases energy called latent heat. Film near the freezing region becomes temporarily warmer than film farther away; it can still be below 0°C. A’s orange upward arrow shows early liquid motion, not heat flow.
cause2: Temperature differences can create differences in the surface’s pull. A liquid interface tends to contract: surface tension. In this study, warmer regions have lower tension than colder ones. Unequal pulls drive liquid along the film: Marangoni flow.
cause3: Flow can detach small crystals from the freezing front and carry them as they grow. In A, cyan trails mark travel and pale branches mark ice. The crystals are on the film, not snow falling inside the bubble.
:::

Why does B’s orange line slow down? This boundary between frozen and liquid regions is the freezing front. The base is cold, but warm air supplies heat and the thin shell conducts heat poorly to the base. The front can therefore stall. B may also have brief flow; this drawing isolates the longer-term front trend. {#warmWhy i18n=text}

::: html step1-5
historyAlt: The warm-air freezing front rises with diagram time, then levels off. A vertical marker shows the current time.
historyNote: Horizontal: 0–24 diagram time. Vertical: B front height above base ÷ bubble diameter (0–1). Only elapsed history is shown. The prescribed orange curve levels off; its limit is neither measured nor universal.
:::

Model limits: fixed environments share one diagram clock; the buttons do not change real temperatures. Paths, counts, speeds and front curves are prescribed. No fluid or heat-transfer equations are solved, and the display gives no real freezing duration or ice coverage. {#s1limit .boundary i18n=text}

## Color does not mean the ice itself is colored {#step2 h2=s2title pre=1}

::: html step2-1
:::

This step asks: where could the colors in the video come from? Several optical effects can share one image. Distinguish these three sources; do not use the image as a thermometer. {#s2body .explain i18n=text}

::: html step2-2
filmTitle: 01 · Remaining liquid film
filmBody: Light reflected from the two film surfaces combines. Different wavelengths reinforce or cancel, producing thin-film interference colors. Thickness and viewing angle affect the result. [[interference#step1|Explore how path difference controls reinforcement and cancellation →]]
iceTitle: 02 · Ice crystals
iceBody: Crystal faces reflect, refract and scatter light. Branches change which light reaches your eye; thin-film interference does not explain every colored feature. [[reflection-refraction#step2|Explore how light changes direction at an interface →]]
lightTitle: 03 · Lighting, background & recording
lightBody: Sunset, sky and background contribute colored light. Exposure, white balance and editing also affect a recording. These principles cannot determine whether the source video was color graded. This lesson’s colors distinguish film, ice and trails only.
limitsTitle: What can this model explain?
limitsBody: It separates seeding, growth, latent-heat release and transport, and compares two freezing regimes. It is not precise CFD (computational fluid dynamics). It predicts no real film temperature, crystal habit, frozen area or freezing duration. Drainage, evaporation, rupture and full optics are omitted. A film on a human-shaped frame can also crystallize, but its boundaries differ from a spherical shell; no quantitative extrapolation is made.
melting: Melting point depends on the recipe. The paper’s glycerol–water–soap mixture melted near −6.5°C. That is not a universal soap-solution value, and pure water’s 0°C is not a universal substitute.
