## Can a Fourier spectrum tell you when? {#step0 h2=s0t}

::: strings
nav0: 0 The blind spot
:::

Step 0 · The blind spot {#s0k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: can the Fourier spectrum alone tell “low tone, then high tone” from “high tone, then low tone”? A **Fourier spectrum** splits a whole signal into sine components at each frequency (in Hz, vibrations per second) and reports how strong each one is. Signal A below plays 12 Hz and then 40 Hz; B is A played backwards in time. Drag “switch time”: both waveforms change, yet in the spectrum the cyan bars (A) and orange dots (B) stay on top of each other. {#s0e .explain}

::: html step0-1
cv0: Signal A, reversed signal B, and their magnitude spectra
s0uL: Switch time (fraction of the 1 s record)
s0reset: Reset switch time
:::

Magnitude spectrum = how large each frequency component is. Playing a real signal backwards leaves the magnitude spectrum unchanged and only alters the phases, so the magnitude spectrum alone cannot tell the order. {#s0term .term i18n=text}

::: strings
s0A: A: 12 Hz first, then 40 Hz
s0B: B: A reversed (40 Hz first, then 12 Hz)
s0Spec: Magnitude spectrum (0–64 Hz)
s0LegA: cyan bars = spectrum of A
s0LegB: orange dots = spectrum of B
s0ro: Switch time {u}: A’s 12 Hz component has strength <b>{lo}</b> and its 40 Hz component <b>{hi}</b> (1 means a unit sine over the whole record). The largest difference between the A and B spectra is <b>{d}</b> (rounding error only), while the RMS difference of the two waveforms is <b>{r}</b> (clearly different).
:::

## Give Fourier a sliding window {#step1 h2=s1t}

::: strings
nav1: 1 Windowing cost
:::

Step 1 · The cost of windowing {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: if we analyse only a short piece of the signal, can we see both when and how many Hz? The **short-time Fourier transform (STFT)** runs a Fourier analysis on just the part inside a window and slides that window along time. The result is a **spectrogram**: time across, frequency up, brighter meaning a stronger component. The window is Gaussian with length L samples (sampling rate 256 samples/s). Drag “window length” with the signal fixed: watch whether the bright band gets thicker or thinner, and how quickly the 12 Hz band fades at the switch at 0.5 s. {#s1e .explain}

::: html step1-1
cv1: Spectrogram of the signal and the window shape
s1LL: Window length L
s1reset: Reset window length
:::

Full width at half maximum = the total width of the band where it is above half its peak brightness; smaller means the frequency is located more precisely (measured on the map). σt = the standard deviation of the Gaussian window, a measure of how wide the window is in time (computed from L, not measured on the map). {#s1term .term i18n=text}

::: strings
s1Guide: dashed = true tones 12 / 40 Hz and the switch time
s1Win: window shape (true duration)
s1ro: Window length <b>{T} ms</b>. Frequency direction: the 12 Hz band at t = 0.25 s has a full width at half maximum of about <b>{w} Hz</b>. Time direction: the window’s time width is σt = <b>{st} ms</b> (computed from L). A longer window pins down frequency better but blurs time; a shorter one does the opposite.
:::

## Wavelets: the window scales with frequency {#step2 h2=s2t}

::: strings
nav2: 2 Stretching wavelet
:::

Step 2 · The stretching wavelet {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: how does a wavelet get a narrow window at high frequency and a wide one at low frequency? A **wavelet** is a short, fading oscillation (here a Morlet wavelet: a Gaussian envelope times a cosine). Pick an analysis frequency f and the wavelet is stretched or squeezed so its envelope always holds about 2 cycles. Slide it along the signal and, at each position, compute a similarity score: the **wavelet coefficient** (shown as a magnitude; 1 = a perfect match to a unit sine). Drag “analysis frequency” with the position fixed: does the orange wavelet get fatter or thinner, and which half does the cyan bump land in? Then drag “wavelet position” or press “Sweep”: the bright line is the part the wavelet has already passed. {#s2e .explain}

::: html step2-1
cv2: Signal, sliding wavelet and wavelet coefficient curve
s2fL: Analysis frequency f
s2tL: Wavelet position
s2reset: Reset and stop
:::

This page always uses a Morlet wavelet with ω₀ = 6: the window width is proportional to 1/f, so the wavelet has the same shape at every frequency and differs only in size. {#s2term .term i18n=text}

::: strings
s2play: ▶ Sweep
s2pause: ⏸ Pause
s2Sig: Signal (12 Hz, then 40 Hz) with the orange wavelet
s2Wav: orange = wavelet at the current position
s2Coef: Wavelet coefficient (bright = swept)
s2ro: Analysis frequency <b>{f} Hz</b> → envelope standard deviation <b>{s} ms</b> (effective window about {d} ms, shorter as f grows). Coefficient at this position: <b>{c}</b>; swept {k} / {n} samples.
:::

## Fixed window vs stretching window {#step3 h2=s3t}

::: strings
nav3: 3 Scalogram
:::

Step 3 · The scalogram {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: given one long low tone and two short high clicks, can both kinds of window show them clearly? The signal is an 8 Hz tone (about 0.1–0.5 s) plus two 48 Hz clicks 80 ms apart (near 0.66 s and 0.74 s). The upper map is the fixed-window spectrogram (STFT); the lower is the wavelet **scalogram** (the wavelet coefficients of every frequency laid out as an image, frequency on a log axis). Drag “STFT window length” and “wavelet cycles ω₀” (a bigger ω₀ puts more cycles inside the envelope) one at a time: does the 8 Hz band get thinner, and do the two clicks separate into two blobs? Each map is scaled to its own brightest cell, so brightness is not compared between maps. {#s3e .explain}

::: html step3-1
cv3: Spectrogram and scalogram of the same signal
s3LL: STFT window length L
s3WL: Wavelet cycles ω₀
s3reset: Reset both parameters
:::

For a Gaussian window the product σt·σf is fixed at 1/(2π): changing the window only moves precision between time and frequency; you cannot shrink both. {#s3term .term i18n=text}

::: strings
s3Stft: Top: fixed-window spectrogram (STFT)
s3Cwt: Bottom: wavelet scalogram (ω₀ adjustable)
s3ro: σ is the standard deviation of a window’s amplitude envelope, a measure of its width. STFT: one window for all frequencies, σt = <b>{st} ms</b>, σf = <b>{sf} Hz</b>. Wavelet: at 8 Hz σt = <b>{t1} ms</b>, σf = <b>{f1} Hz</b>; at 48 Hz σt = <b>{t2} ms</b>, σf = <b>{f2} Hz</b>. Rule of thumb: the two clicks separate fairly easily once the window’s σt is below about half their 80 ms gap.
:::

## Describe a sharp change with the fewest numbers {#step4 h2=s4t}

::: strings
nav4: 4 Haar & sparsity
:::

Step 4 · Haar and sparsity {#s4k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: how many coefficients does a signal with sharp jumps really need? Expand the signal in an orthonormal basis (the Haar wavelet basis, or the Fourier cosine/sine basis, 128 coefficients each), keep only the K coefficients with the largest magnitude, set the rest to zero, and rebuild. **RMSE (root-mean-square error)**: square the point-by-point difference between the rebuilt signal and the noise-free original, average, take the square root; smaller is better. Drag “kept coefficients K”: compare orange (Haar) with cyan (Fourier) at the pulse edges and see which RMSE falls first. Then drag “noise”: the grey line gets rough, but keeping only a few large coefficients filters part of it out. {#s4e .explain}

::: html step4-1
cv4: Original signal and sparse rebuilds in two bases
s4KL: Kept coefficients K
s4NL: Noise standard deviation
s4reset: Reset K and noise
:::

The signal is a weak sine plus a wide pulse over samples 45–76 and a narrow pulse over samples 100–103; the wide pulse has odd-numbered edges (45, 77), so Haar gets no alignment advantage. For very small K Fourier is slightly ahead; as K grows Haar overtakes it (the gap narrows when noise is large). {#s4term .term i18n=text}

::: strings
s4In: Input (grey = noisy, green = noise-free original)
s4H: Haar rebuild (RMSE {e}, green = original)
s4F: Fourier rebuild (RMSE {e}, green = original)
s4Leg: Horizontal axis: 128 samples; both rebuilds keep exactly K coefficients.
s4ro: Keeping <b>{K} / {n}</b> coefficients, noise standard deviation {sd}. The input itself has RMSE <b>{ri}</b> against the original; Haar rebuild <b>{rh}</b>; Fourier rebuild <b>{rf}</b>; currently smaller: <b>{who}</b>. This holds only for this page’s signal: for a pure sine, Fourier would win with just 1 coefficient.

Step 5 · Finale {#s5k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The thread of this page: a Fourier spectrum answers “what is in the signal”; windows and wavelets let it also answer “when”; sparse representation asks “what is the fewest numbers that describe it”. {#s5e .explain i18n=text}

::: html step5-1
c1t: Spectra carry no order
c1b: The magnitude spectrum is unchanged by time reversal, so low-then-high and high-then-low share a spectrum (step 0; real signals, magnitude only).
c2t: The window trade-off
c2b: A longer window sharpens frequency and blurs time; for a Gaussian window σt·σf stays at 1/(2π) (steps 1 and 3).
c3t: A window that scales
c3b: A wavelet keeps σf/f = 1/ω₀ fixed: high frequencies get fine time location, low frequencies get fine frequency location (steps 2 and 3).
c4t: Sparsity depends on the signal
c4b: For this page’s noise-free signal the jumps cost fewer Haar coefficients; a smooth periodic signal costs fewer Fourier coefficients. There is no universal winner (step 4).
modelT: Model and limits
modelB: Everything here is a discrete teaching calculation: 256 Hz sampling, 256 points (128 in step 4); a Gaussian window or a Morlet wavelet (ω₀ = 6 by default); zeros are assumed outside the record, so coefficients near the edges are too small. The maps are illustrations, not instrument measurements, and the Haar comparison covers one hand-built signal.
:::
