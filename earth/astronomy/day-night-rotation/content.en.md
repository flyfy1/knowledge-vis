## Lights on: one half bright, one half dark {#step1 h2=s1t}

::: strings
nav1: 1 Lights on
:::

Step 1 · Lights on {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Question: once we switch the “lamp” on, why is only half of the Earth lit? Where is the line between light and dark, and what decides it?<br>The Sun is so far away that its light reaches us in parallel lines, like a row of arrows marching side by side — this is called **parallel light**; only the half of the sphere facing those arrows can be lit.<br>The line between the lit half and the dark half is called the **terminator**: anyone standing on it is watching either a sunrise or a sunset (step 2, once the Earth turns, makes clear which is which). In the side view below, the terminator is computed from the direction the sunlight comes from — it does not move when you change your viewing angle. {#s1e .explain}

::: html step1-1
sideCap: Side view (looking from the side; sunlight comes in level from the right)
sideLabel: Side view: the circle is the Earth, the Sun is on the right, the line between light and dark is the terminator
:::

Takeaway: the half facing the Sun is daytime, the half facing away is night, and the dividing line is the terminator. It is fixed by the direction of the sunlight — turning your view only moves your eyes, never the line. {#s1result .readout i18n=text}

::: strings
lightOffBtn: Lights off (take the Sun away)
lightOnBtn: Lights on (bring the Sun back)
s1roOn: With the light on: the half of the Earth facing the Sun is in daytime, the half facing away is in night. However you drag the 3D view around, that dividing line only ever follows the direction of the sunlight.
s1roOff: With the light off: there is no Sun, and the whole Earth is dark — without sunlight, there is no difference between day and night at all.
:::

Step 2 · Start turning {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Question: once the Earth itself starts turning, what does the figure go through, in order? At what times does his sky get dark, and at what times does it get light?<br>The Earth spinning around its own axis is called **rotation**. The speed in the picture is just animation speed; one full turn stands for a real 24 hours.<br>First, names for two moments: when the figure **faces the Sun squarely**, that moment is called **noon**, written as 12:00; when he **faces exactly away**, that moment is **midnight**, 0:00. Press play and watch him pass through dusk, into night, past midnight, into dawn and sunrise, and back to noon; the time band below colours in each stretch of time as it passes. {#s2e .explain}

Step 3 · A different viewpoint {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Question: the Earth is the thing turning — so why, standing on the ground, does the Sun look like the thing that moves, rising in the east and setting in the west?<br>Standing on the ground, the line where the sky you can see meets the ground is called the **horizon**. The sky view on the right draws the sky exactly as the figure sees it: the path the Sun seems to take through his sky is computed from the same turning angle as the space view on the left, at the same moment. ## Which way does it turn — and how long is one turn? {#step4 h2=s4t}

::: strings
nav4: 4 Which way does it turn?
:::

Step 4 · Direction and period {#s4k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Question: which way does the Earth actually turn, how can we know, and what would happen if it turned the other way?<br>First, a promise from step 0 to keep: this page holds the Sun still and turns only the Earth because day and night depend only on which side faces the Sun — turning either one would show the same thing, and turning the Earth itself is what lets us study the rotation.<br>One more new word: while it rotates, the Earth also travels around the Sun, taking one year per lap — that is called **revolution**; revolution plus a tilted axis is what makes the seasons, which is another page's story, not this one's. {#s4e .explain}

Takeaway: the Earth rotates from west to east (counterclockwise, seen from above the North Pole), and one full rotation is one day — 24 hours. The faster it turned, the shorter a day would be. {#s4result .readout i18n=text}

::: strings
revBtn: Imagine it: turn the Earth backwards
revBackBtn: Back to the real direction (west to east)
s4roNorm: The real direction: looking down from above the North Pole, the Earth turns counterclockwise — that is what “from west to east” means. The Sun rises in the east, just as we see every day.
s4roRev: The imagined backwards direction: look at the figure's sky view — the Sun now rises in the west! But in the real world the Sun rises in the east, so the real rotation can only be from west to east.
:::

model2: ② There is another way to measure “one turn”: this page's 24 hours is measured against the Sun (a solar day). Measured against the distant stars, one turn actually takes 23 hours 56 minutes; the missing few minutes come from revolution, which this page does not open up.
model3: ③ Sizes, distances and continent shapes are schematic (as step 0 said). But drawing the sunlight as parallel lines is a serious approximation, not a shortcut: the Sun is so far away that its light arrives at the Earth almost perfectly parallel.
