## Growing Left: The Other Side of 0 {#step0 h2=s0t}

::: strings
nav0: 0 Growing Left
:::

Step 0 · Completing the Line {#s0k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The question for this step: **is there really nothing to the left of 0? What should the ticks over there say?** You already know the number line: a number is a point on the line, and 0 is the **origin**, the border between the two sides. To the right of 0 live the **positive numbers** — the ordinary numbers you write every day, whose “+” sign can be left out. Press “Grow left” and watch the line grow one tick at a time: the new numbers on the left are the **negative numbers**, written with a “−” in front. Click any tick to see exactly how it relates to the tick opposite it. {#s0e .explain}

::: html step0-1
s0extLab: Grow this many ticks left
s0play: Grow left ▶
s0reset: Take it back
:::

See it? The left side is not empty — it is the **mirror image** of the right: every tick that grows on the left has a partner opposite it, equally far from 0. So “−” does not mean “less of something”; it records **direction: which side of 0 you are on**. This page labels whole-number ticks only; negative decimals and fractions live between the ticks, by the same logic, and you will meet them later. {#s0hint .hint}

:::

## The Thermometer: Negatives Can Be Compared {#step1 h2=s1t}

::: strings
nav1: 1 Thermometer Sizes
:::

Step 1 · Comparing Negatives {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The question for this step: **−5 or −1 — which is colder?** First, know your tool: a thermometer is a **number line stood upright**. The scale mark level with the top of the liquid is the reading, and the unit is **degrees Celsius (℃)**. Temperatures below 0℃ are called **below zero**: “5 below zero” is written −5℃. Drag the liquid column (or use the sliders and preset buttons) to set Temperature A and Temperature B, then press “Compare” — the page projects both temperatures back onto a horizontal number line and judges by position. Watch out for the classic trap: “5 is bigger than 1, so −5 must be warmer than −1.” The landing points are about to dismantle it. {#s1e .explain}

::: html step1-1
s1aLab: Temperature A (℃)
s1bLab: Temperature B (℃)
s1tuneLab: Presets apply to:
s1tuneA: A
s1tuneB: B
s1p5: 5℃ above zero
s1pM1: 1℃ below zero
s1pM5: 5℃ below zero
s1cmp: Compare
:::

Dismantling the trap: the “5” inside −5 only says it is 5 ticks away from 0; a bigger digit part does not mean a bigger number. Comparing negatives takes exactly one look: **whoever sits further left on the number line is smaller**. −5 is left of −1, so −5 is smaller and colder. This works everywhere on this page: further left is smaller, further right is bigger. {#s1hint .hint}

## The Elevator: Above and Below Ground {#step2 h2=s2t}

::: strings
nav2: 2 Elevator Floors
:::

Step 2 · Positions Carry Signs {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The question for this step: **can floors be a number line too? What number records a floor underground?** This building sets its ground floor as 0 (like the origin of the line, like 0℃ on the thermometer), with +1, +2, +3 above and −1, −2 below. Shops write **B1, B2** for the basement floors: B stands for basement, so B1 is floor −1 and B2 is floor −2. Press the up/down buttons, or click the floor you want, and watch what happens to the floor number as the elevator crosses the ground. {#s2e .explain}

::: html step2-1
s2nLab: Floors per ride
s2up: ▲ Go up
s2down: ▼ Go down
s2reset: Back to ground, clear trail
:::

The ground is this building’s 0. When the elevator rides down through 0, the floor numbers turn negative — exactly the same event as a temperature dropping through 0℃ or a point walking left of the origin: fix a 0 first, call one direction positive and the other negative, and any position can be recorded with a signed number. This step only records positions and ride results; addition and subtraction formulas get on the line properly in Step 3. {#s2hint .hint}

## Walking the Line: Addition and Subtraction Are Footsteps {#step3 h2=s3t}

::: strings
nav3: 3 Walking the Line
:::

Step 3 · Adding Is Walking {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The question for this step: **where does an addition or subtraction with negative numbers land? Can you walk it out instead of memorising rules?** There is just one rule: **addition = walk right, subtraction = walk left**. Set the start, the direction and the number of steps, then press “Walk →”: the walker crosses the line one tick at a time, the route leaves a **trail**, and the tick it stops on is the **landing point**. Keep an eye on the origin (the position of 0) in the picture — count from it as you walk and you will not get lost. {#s3e .explain}

Mini-demo: Taking Back a Walk to the Left {#s3uTitle .subhead tag=h3 i18n=text}

::: html step3-2

The same story in weather language: the forecast says tonight will drop by −2 degrees (say it loosely for now: it means the temperature is to fall 2 degrees); later the drop is cancelled — and “cancelling a drop” is a rise. Taking back a move in the opposite direction is doing the same amount in the forward direction. Turning this into a written rule for equations is a middle-school matter; this page shows the phenomenon only, no rules to memorise. {#s3hint .hint}

## Negative Numbers Were Invented for “The Other Direction” {#step5 h2=s5t}

::: strings
nav5: 5 Finale
:::

Step 5 · The Finale {#s5k .step-kicker i18n=text}

The question for this step: **was this page about one thing, or three?** Look at the three little pictures below: the horizontal number line, the upright thermometer, the elevator inside its building — one and the same line wearing three outfits. Every time, we first fix a 0, then record one direction as positive and the opposite direction as negative. {#s5e .explain}

::: html step5-1
s5fin: The one-sentence summary: **quantities have directions — fix a 0 first, record one direction as positive and the opposite as negative.** Temperatures and floors are recorded this way; so are altitude (with sea level as 0) and income versus spending (with breaking even as 0). Negative numbers are not “numbers you owe” — they are the notation invented for the other direction, and there were always numbers on the other side of 0.

:::
