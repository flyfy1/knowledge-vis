## How can a list of numbers stand for a word? {#step0 h2=s0t}

::: strings
nav0: 0 Words as numbers
:::

Step 0 · Words as numbers {#s0k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: why can a word be turned into a list of numbers whose positions carry meaning? A **word embedding** gives every word a fixed-length list of numbers. This page starts with hand-set toy features: five numbers per word for gender, royalty, age, human and alive. Pick two features as the horizontal and vertical axes and the words become points on a plane: set the horizontal axis to “gender” and the vertical axis to “royalty” and see how king/queen and man/woman mirror each other; then change axes and see which words land on top of each other. Pick a word to read its full vector. Real embeddings are learned by machines from large amounts of text and their dimensions have no such tidy names; the hand-set features are only here to make the idea visible. {#s0e .explain}

## How do we measure how similar two words are? {#step1 h2=s1t}

::: strings
nav1: 1 Measuring similarity
:::

Step 1 · Measuring similarity {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: which quantity tells us whether two word vectors are alike? The **dot product** A·B is the sum of the per-dimension products (the bars in the third row below added up). The **cosine similarity** cos = A·B / (|A||B|) looks only at the angle between the vectors, runs from −1 to 1 and ignores length. The **Euclidean distance** is the straight-line distance between the vectors’ endpoints and does depend on length. Pick word A and word B, then drag “scale B” with A fixed: B’s bars grow, the cosine does not move at all, and the distance changes. At the bottom is the cosine of A with every word (orange = the B you picked). {#s1e .explain}

::: html step1-1
s1aL: Word A
s1bL: Word B
cv1: Dimension bars of words A and B, per-dimension products and the similarity ranking
s1kL: Scale B (times)
s1reset: Reset
:::

|A| = vector length = √(sum of squares of all dimensions). A cosine of 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular, −1 means opposite. {#s1term .term i18n=text}

## Why does king − man + woman land on queen? {#step2 h2=s2t}

::: strings
nav2: 2 Analogy arithmetic
:::

Step 2 · Analogy arithmetic {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: why can adding and subtracting vectors solve analogies? Every word is a point on the plot. The difference between king and man (solid orange arrow) is the direction “from ordinary person to royalty”; slide that arrow over to woman (dashed orange) and its tip is woman plus the same “royalty” change, which lands exactly on queen. Formula: result = A − λ·B + λ·C (λ = 1 is the full analogy). Pick three words and drag λ: the white dot walks from A toward the answer, and the readout names the closest word (highest cosine); note that when you have not gone far enough the nearest word may be prince, and only the full arrow ends at queen. The three input words are not allowed as candidates (the usual convention; otherwise the answer is often an input itself). The plot shows only the first two dimensions, while the nearest word is computed over all five. {#s2e .explain}

λ is a “how far” factor added by this page so you can see the process; it is not part of a model. The standard analogy uses λ = 1. {#s2term .term i18n=text}

Step 3 · The nearest word {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: what does analogy arithmetic actually return? First a **point**, and then the page looks up the word closest to it in the vocabulary. Use the checkboxes to choose which words are in the vocabulary, with king − man + woman fixed: untick queen first and see who becomes the answer and how high its cosine is; then untick princess and girl and keep watching. Bar height is the cosine of each candidate with the result point. Take-away: the answer depends on the vocabulary and is not “the truth”; even a high cosine only says the directions are close. (The three input words king, man and woman are not candidates.) {#s3e .explain}

## If nobody writes features by hand, where do vectors come from? {#step4 h2=s4t}

::: strings
nav4: 4 Look at neighbours
:::

Step 4 · Look at the neighbours {#s4k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: how can a machine get word vectors on its own? The core idea is “look at who a word appears with”. Below is a mini corpus of only 14 sentences. Drag “sentences read” (or press “Read sentence by sentence”): each sentence read adds 1 to the table for every pair of neighbouring words. The **window** is how far to the left and right counts as “neighbouring” (sentences have only 3 words, so a window above 2 changes nothing). Each row is a target word’s **co-occurrence count vector** (columns are neighbour words); compare two rows with the cosine from step 1: the words with the most similar neighbours are the most similar words. Pick a target word, change the window and watch the bars below. This uses raw counts and is not real training; methods such as word2vec and GloVe compress this kind of statistic into dense vectors of a few hundred dimensions on huge corpora. {#s4e .explain}

::: html step4-1
s4tL: Target word

s4kL: Sentences read
s4winL: Window size
s4reset: Reset
:::

Co-occurrence count = how often two words appear together within the window; two words with identical neighbour distributions have cosine 1. The corpus is tiny, so this is only a demonstration. {#s4term .term i18n=text}

## Wrap-up: words as a computable map {#step5 h2=s5t}

::: strings
nav5: 5 Finale
:::

Step 5 · Finale {#s5k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Word embeddings turn words into numbers we can measure distances between and add and subtract; this page goes from hand-set features to “look at the neighbours”, showing that such numbers can be designed or learned from text. {#s5e .explain i18n=text}

::: html step5-1
c1t: A word = a list of numbers
c1b: One number per dimension, so a word becomes a point in space (step 0; the features here are hand-set).
c2t: Cosine looks at direction
c2b: Cosine compares only direction and ignores length; Euclidean distance depends on length too (step 1).
c3t: Analogy = arithmetic + nearest
c3b: Vector arithmetic gives a point and the answer is the nearest word in the vocabulary, which depends on the vocabulary (steps 2 and 3).
c4t: Vectors can be learned
c4b: Count who appears with whom and words with similar neighbours get similar vectors (step 4).
modelT: Model and limits
modelB: Steps 0–3 use hand-set 5-dimensional toy features whose axes all have names; real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions with no clear meaning on any axis, analogies do not always work, and they absorb biases from the text. Step 4 is raw co-occurrence counting plus cosine, not the training procedure of word2vec or GloVe; the mini corpus has only 14 sentences, so the result is a demonstration.
:::
