## How does a word decide whom to look at? {#step0 h2=s0t}

::: strings
nav0: 0 Query & key
:::

Step 0 · Query and key {#s0k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: how does one word (the query) score every other word for relevance? Each word has a **key vector** (coloured arrow: “what I am”). The word asking sends out a **query vector** (white arrow: “what I am looking for”). Their **dot product** q·k = |q||k|cosθ is the relevance score: the more aligned the directions the higher the score, and opposite directions score negative. First pick the asking word, then drag “rotate query”: with the length fixed, which key does it point at first, and how do the bars change? Then drag “stretch query”: the direction stays and scores scale up in proportion. The vectors are hand-set 2-D toy data, not trained. {#s0e .explain}

Dot product = multiply matching components and add; its size depends on both the angle (direction) and the lengths. {#s0term .term i18n=text}

## How do scores become “how much to look”? {#step1 h2=s1t}

::: strings
nav1: 1 Scores to weights
:::

Step 1 · Scores to weights {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: scores can be positive or negative, so how do they become attention weights that add up to 1? **Softmax** turns each score s into e^(β·s) (always positive; β is the sharpness) and divides by the total. The top chart shows the raw scores of “sat” against every word (the default query from step 0); the bottom chart shows the weights. Drag “sharpness β” with query and keys fixed: as β grows, the top-scoring word takes almost all the weight; as β → 0, the weights approach an even split (1/6 each). β = 0.71 is the standard choice 1/√d_k (here d_k = 2; step 4 explains why we divide by √d_k). {#s1e .explain}

s1reset: Reset sharpness
:::

Entropy = how spread out the weights are: largest when all are equal, 0 when one word takes everything (unit: bits; smaller = more concentrated). {#s1term .term i18n=text}

## How do weights become an output? {#step2 h2=s2t}

::: strings
nav2: 2 Weighted sum
:::

Step 2 · The weighted sum {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: once we know “who to look at more”, what is this word’s new representation? Each word also has a **value vector** (thin arrow: “the content I can offer”). The output is the weighted sum of the values: o = Σ wⱼ·vⱼ. The thick arrows chain “weight × value” head to tail, and the white dot is the output. Drag “query direction” (length fixed, standard 1/√d_k scaling): watch how the weight bars and the arrow chain change; the green line is the path the output has traced; press “Sweep” to turn through a full circle automatically, the bright part being what has been covered. Because the weights are non-negative and sum to 1, the output always stays inside the region spanned by the value vectors. {#s2e .explain}

Step 3 · The sentence matrix {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: if all six words ask at the same time, what table do we get? Each row is an asking word, each column a word being looked at, and each cell is a weight (rows add up to 1; brighter = larger). Drag “rotate all queries”: the keys stay put, which stands in for changing how queries are produced (a teaching substitute for trained weight matrices), and the pattern rearranges. Press “causal mask”: each word may only see itself and the words before it (how a language model generates word by word); masked cells get weight 0 and the rest are renormalised. ## Why divide by √d_k? {#step4 h2=s4t}

::: strings
nav4: 4 Why divide by √d
:::

Step 4 · Why divide by √d {#s4k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: what happens to dot products when the dimension d grows? For random vectors q and k (each component standard normal), the dot product has a standard deviation of about √d. The larger d is, the more spread out the scores, and the easier softmax collapses onto one word; softmax then saturates and its gradient becomes tiny (this page shows only how concentrated the weights get, not training). Dividing the scores by √d brings the standard deviation back to about 1. Drag “dimension d” with everything else fixed: the orange histogram (unscaled, axis fixed at ±64) widens while the cyan one (scaled, axis fixed at ±4) barely changes, and in the single trial below the orange bar may take almost everything. The data come from 300 random trials of 8 keys each. {#s4e .explain}

Standard deviation = the typical size of deviations from the mean. Random trials wobble a little; √d is the expected value. {#s4term .term i18n=text}

## Wrap-up: three steps to a word’s new representation {#step5 h2=s5t}

::: strings
nav5: 5 Finale
:::

Step 5 · Finale {#s5k .step-kicker i18n=text}

How a word “looks around”: score with the dot product of query and key, turn scores into weights with softmax, then take the weighted sum of the values. {#s5e .explain i18n=text}

::: html step5-1
c1t: Dot product = relevance
c1b: The more query and key point the same way, the higher the score; length scales the score too (step 0).
c2t: Softmax = weights
c2b: Scores become non-negative weights that sum to 1; a larger β concentrates them (step 1).
c3t: Weighted sum = output
c3b: The output is a weighted average of the values and stays inside the region they span (step 2).
c4t: Scaling and masking
c4b: Dividing by √d_k keeps the weights from collapsing onto one word in high dimension; the causal mask lets each word see only itself and earlier words (steps 3 and 4).
modelT: Model and limits
modelB: Everything here is a hand-set 2-D toy (d_k = 2), not trained Q, K and V; real models use high-dimensional vectors, several attention heads, and linear projections learned from data. Attention weights describe how one layer mixes information; they are not a complete explanation of why a model gave its output. Step 4 is a random simulation, so its numbers wobble with the seed.
:::
