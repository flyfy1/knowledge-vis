## How curvy should the curve be? {#step0 h2=s0t}

::: strings
nav0: 0 Fit a curve
:::

Step 0 · Fit a curve {#s0k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: when fitting 12 noisy points with a polynomial, how “bendy” should the curve be? The larger the **polynomial degree** d, the more flexible the curve (a degree-d polynomial has d+1 adjustable coefficients). The points are the true curve y = sin(2πx) (white dashed) plus random noise (standard deviation 0.25); the orange curve is the **least-squares** fit (it minimises the sum of squared vertical errors from the points to the curve). Drag the degree d: at d = 0 or 1 the curve is too stiff to follow the wiggle (underfitting); at d = 11 it passes through every point yet swings wildly between them (overfitting). The **training RMSE** in the readout is the curve’s average error (root-mean-square) on these 12 points; smaller means it hugs the training points more closely. {#s0e .explain}

:::

RMSE = square root of the mean squared error, in the same unit as y. Training RMSE only says how closely the curve matches data it has already seen. {#s0term .term i18n=text}

Step 1 · Training error and test error {#s1k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: if the error on the training points is smaller, is the error on unseen points smaller too? The page keeps 300 extra **test points** that never took part in the fit (the same true curve, the same noise size). The cyan line is the training RMSE at each degree, the orange line the test RMSE, and the green dashed line is the noise’s own standard deviation: even knowing the true curve exactly, the test error can only fall to about this line. Step 2 · More data, more stability {#s2k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: keep a very flexible curve (d = 9) and increase the number of samples n; what happens? Drag “training points n”: the upper picture is the curve fitted to n points and the lower one is the **learning curve**: n across, training RMSE in cyan, test RMSE in orange. With very small n the orange line shoots off the chart (the curve swings between the points); as n grows the two lines move together toward the noise line, showing that enough data can hold a flexible model in check. Note: the points for each n are regenerated from a fixed seed, so the numbers for a given n depend on that seed. {#s2e .explain}

## Same model, a different batch of data: what changes? {#step3 h2=s3t}

::: strings
nav3: 3 New samples
:::

Step 3 · New samples {#s3k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: why is a high-degree fit “unstable”? Each press of the button refits on a fresh batch of 12 noisy points; the thin orange lines pile up on the chart and the green line is their average. Drag the degree d: for small d every fit is similar but the whole family sits away from the true curve (large **bias**); for large d the average is close to the true curve but the individual fits all differ and scatter widely (large **variance**). The readout gives both numbers: bias² = the mean over the grid of the squared gap between the average fit and the true curve; variance = the mean of the squared deviation of each fit from the average fit (both computed on 41 grid points between 0 and 1, and defined only from 2 batches on). {#s3e .explain}

Bias² measures “how far off the average is”, variance “how much it wobbles from batch to batch”; at most 20 batches, so the numbers fluctuate randomly. {#s3term .term i18n=text}

## Can we keep the curve in check without lowering the degree? {#step4 h2=s4t}

::: strings
nav4: 4 Regularisation
:::

Step 4 · Regularisation {#s4k .step-kicker i18n=text}

This step answers: without lowering the degree, how do we stop a flexible curve from swinging? **Ridge regression (L2 regularisation)** minimises the sum of squared errors and also penalises the size of the coefficients: objective = Σ(error)² + λ·Σ(coefficient)². The larger λ, the smaller the coefficients and the smoother the curve. Fix d = 11 and 12 points and drag λ (log scale): with a tiny λ the curve passes through every point (training error 0) and swings; a moderate λ cuts the test error sharply; a huge λ flattens the curve and underfits again. The white dot is the λ with the lowest test error in the scanned range. {#s4e .explain}

λ is a penalty strength we choose (a hyperparameter) and should be picked with data that did not take part in the fit; this page picks it with the same test points, whereas real work keeps a separate validation set. {#s4term .term i18n=text}

## Wrap-up: flexibility should be just right {#step5 h2=s5t}

::: strings
nav5: 5 Finale

Step 5 · Finale {#s5k .step-kicker i18n=text}

Training error only falls with flexibility, while error on unseen data falls and then rises; data size, regularisation and knowing how much a fit wobbles from sample to sample are the three handles to control it. {#s5e .explain i18n=text}

Real problems also need a validation set or cross-validation, and have far more complex models and noise.
