## The proton count decides what it is {#step1 h2=s1t h2part=1}

::: html step1-1
s1k: STEP 1 · IDENTITY
s1e: This step answers: what makes carbon carbon, and oxygen oxygen? The nucleus holds two kinds of particle: **protons** (particles with positive charge) and **neutrons** (particles with no charge that only add weight). Count the protons and you get the **nuclear charge number** (each proton carries one unit of positive charge, so the nucleus's total positive charge is exactly the proton count). Use the − / + buttons to add or remove protons one at a time, and watch the element's name change.
s1bridge: Press +1 once: adding one proton immediately changes the element. A neutron changes the mass, not the element’s identity.
zLab: Protons Z (nuclear charge number)
s1hint: This page stops at calcium: Z is clamped to 1…20. Names and symbols are data from a table, but the identity itself is not a table lookup — whatever Z is, that is the element with that number.
:::

::: strings
s1ro: Protons Z = {z} → this is <b>{name}</b> (symbol {sym}). Neutrons N = {n} (the most common neutron count for this element); mass number A = Z + N = {a} (the mass number is just the total count of particles in the nucleus). For now the electrons match the protons: {e} (a neutral atom — Step 4 will change that). Neutrons only change the mass number A, never the identity: identity is decided by protons alone.
:::

## Electrons sit in layers — each layer has a fixed capacity {#step2 h2=s2t h2part=1}

::: html step2-1
s2k: STEP 2 · ARRANGEMENT
s2e: This step answers: how do the electrons arrange themselves outside the nucleus — in a lump, or by rules? Pick an atom first (a preset, or Z − / +); the electron count starts equal to the proton count. Then add or remove electrons one at a time with the e − / + buttons: the page seats the electrons **one by one into electron layers** (the concentric zones electrons roughly live in) — an inner layer fills completely before the next one starts. Watch where each electron lands, then check the arrangement formula below.
s2bridge: Pick Na or Cl, then press electron + / − once: the inner layer fills first, and only then does the outer arrangement change.
presetLab: Preset atoms (picking one also sets e = Z):
eLab: Electrons e
fillBtn: Replay the seating
s2model: Two model notes: (1) The rings are only the electrons' **address zones** — a diagram convention. Electrons do not actually run along the rings; once seated, an electron only shifts a little within its own layer. (2) The 2 / 8 / 8 capacities are a **simplified arrangement rule for middle school**; the real rules are finer and come later. Within this page's range (Z and e up to 20), this rule reproduces the textbook arrangements.
:::

## The outermost layer decides how reactive it is {#step3 h2=s3t h2part=1}

::: html step3-1
s3k: STEP 3 · TEMPERAMENT
s3e: This step answers: which number in the arrangement runs the show — why is neon aloof while sodium flares up? Click through the comparison set. The readout reports only three things: the **outermost layer count** (the last number of the arrangement), whether that layer is full, and how many are missing; the outermost layer is highlighted on the diagram. The wording is deliberately 'tends to': this is a qualitative judgement from fullness, not a promise that a reaction will happen.
s3bridge: Check the outer layer first: a full layer is more stable; a gap shows how many electrons are easier to gain or lose.

:::

## After electrons come and go: ions {#step4 h2=s4t h2part=1}

::: html step4-1
s4k: STEP 4 · IONS
s4e: This step answers: what does an atom become when it loses or gains electrons? Pick an atom, then drag the electron slider. The total charge is fixed by one formula: **charge = protons − electrons** (each proton counts +1, each electron −1). An atom carrying a net charge is an **ion**: positive ones are cations, negative ones are anions. Watch the arrangement on the diagram and the charge at the symbol's top-right corner change together.
s4bridge: Compare He, Ne, Na and Cl in order: full outer layers are stable; the remaining gap makes the gain-or-loss direction easier to see.
preset4Lab: Jump straight to an ion:
:::

::: strings
s4ro: {sym}: protons {z}, electrons {e}, arrangement {cfg} (an ion's arrangement = the electrons that remain, re-layered). Charge = {z} − {e} = <b>{q}</b>. {kind}
kindNeutral: Charge 0: still a neutral atom, not an ion.
kindCation: Positively charged: a cation, written {ion}.
kindAnion: Negatively charged: an anion, written {ion}.
kindBare: All electrons gone: only the bare nucleus remains — the protons are still there.
s4explore: Exploration zone: this many electrons puts more than 8 in the fourth layer — beyond what the simplified 2 / 8 / 8 middle-school rule can express. Here the arrangement string only serves to count the electrons so the charge comes out right; do not read it as the real layering — the real rules are finer and come later.
:::

## Quiz: four questions, all marked by the same rules {#step5 h2=s5t h2part=1}

dg_q3_stable8: 8 is layer 2's full capacity, not sodium's outer layer. The arrangement 2, 8, 1 ends in 1.
dg_q3_lose8: Half right on direction (it does tend to lose), wrong on the count: it would lose the 1 outermost electron, not 8.
