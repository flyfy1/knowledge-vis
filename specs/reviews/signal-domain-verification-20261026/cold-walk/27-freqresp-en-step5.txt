High-pass, band-pass, resonance: one mathematics, three appetites

Same R and C — just take the output across the resistor instead, and the low-pass becomes a high-pass on the spot: |H(jω)| = ωτ/√(1+(ωτ)²). Low frequencies are blocked, highs pass, and the −3 dB point sits at the same ωc = 1/τ. Drag τ and watch the cutoff run; compare with step 2’s low-pass Bode plot — the two are mirror images.

τ = 1.00 s · ωc = 1.00 rad/s · at ωc, |H| = 0.7071 and φ = +45.0° (the high-pass leads where the low-pass lags — same magnitude, opposite sign) · at ω = 0.1ωc, |H| = 0.0995 (lows cut to about a tenth)

One more step: put a low-pass and a high-pass back to back so only a middle band of frequencies passes — a band-pass. The standard second-order band-pass H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jQ(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)) peaks at ω₀ with height 1, and the quality factor Q sets how fat the peak is — larger Q, sharper peak. One readout below is a live audit: the half-power bandwidth Δω measured off the curve (the distance between the two points where |H| falls to 1/√2 of the peak) must satisfy Q = ω₀/Δω — whether the measured number matches the slider is for the digits to say. This is a normalized band-pass output. A spring’s displacement/force response has a different numerator, so its peak location and height cannot be copied from this curve. Tuning a radio is band-pass work: the peak aims at the station, and Q decides whether the neighboring station bleeds in. Too small a Q and two stations mush together; too large and the peak is so sharp it shaves the station’s own high frequencies. Even pickiness has to be portioned right.
