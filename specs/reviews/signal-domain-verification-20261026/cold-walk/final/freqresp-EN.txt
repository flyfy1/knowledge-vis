Frequency Response & Filters · The Picky Box

An LTI box answers a sine wave with a sine wave of the same frequency — only scaled, only delayed. Plot the pass rate |H(ω)| and the lateness φ(ω) against frequency and you have the box’s entire character: its frequency response. It is both a system’s medical report and a filter’s blueprint — filtering is just being picky by frequency. Every curve on this page is measured and computed live.

Step 0 · The Hook

A box that is picky by frequency

Start with a box of unknown principle. The top trace is a slow, clean sine wave buried under a layer of fast, fine noise. Feed it through the box and look at the output — most of the noise is gone, the slow wave is almost untouched. The box has never seen this waveform. How does it know what to keep and what to throw away? Who splits a signal into these frequencies: the spectrum step on the Fourier page →

The answer: it judges by frequency alone. Below, feed it pure sine waves one notch at a time: the higher the frequency, the shorter the output. Measure each frequency once, record “output amplitude ÷ input amplitude” as a point — sweep through them all and the box’s menu appears.

A convention, stated where it belongs: the output waveform above is drawn by analytic steady-state superposition (each component scaled by the closed-form |H| and shifted by the closed-form φ), while the pass-rate readouts below are measured by an ODE time-domain simulation — two different engines on one screen; step 1 sets them side by side as two cards and audits them against each other.

This box is in fact the hero of the whole page: a first-order RC low-pass. Its pass rates across frequencies form the curve called the frequency response |H(ω)|; how much it delays each frequency is a second curve, φ(ω). This page measures both curves, teaches you to read them, and then turns them around to design filters.

Step 1 · What a Frequency Response Is

Sine in, sine out: only two things change

Open the box: a resistor R and a capacitor C, output taken across the capacitor — the classic first-order RC low-pass. Feed it cos(ωt) and, once the transient dies out, the output is still a sine of the same frequency, with only two changes: its amplitude is multiplied by |H(ω)|, and its phase is pushed back by φ(ω). The two cards below record the answer at the current frequency side by side: one computed from the formula (the analytic card), one measured off the simulated waveform with an oscilloscope mindset (the measured card: the page really integrates the circuit equation with RK4, then projects the steady-state waveform to extract amplitude and phase). If the two cards disagree, neither can be trusted. Drag ω through the range; every frequency you visit lights a point on the |H| curve below.

H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC), τ = RC = 1 s, ωc = 1/τ = 1 rad/s

Look closely at ω = ωc = 1: |H| is exactly 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 and φ is exactly −45°. This frequency is the cutoff, and the next step’s whole Bode plot revolves around it. One definition, stated plainly: “measured” means steady state — the brief switch-on transient does not count. The simulation lets the circuit run for 6τ plus several periods before measuring, and the reviewers use the same convention (the method is printed under the measured card).

Step 2 · The Bode Plot

The Bode plot: how engineers read it

Put the two curves from step 1 on a logarithmic frequency axis, with amplitude in decibels (dB = 20·log₁₀|H|), and you get the Bode plot engineers stare at all day. Drag R and C and the cutoff ωc = 1/(RC) moves with them: the −3 dB point (where |H| = 1/√2) is marked on the magnitude plot, the high-frequency asymptote hugs the curve at −20 dB/dec (every 10× in frequency costs 20 dB), and φ(ωc) = −45° is marked on the phase plot — all three annotations are computed live from the current R and C.

The Bode trick: the curve is flat at 0 dB at low frequency, then past ωc it hugs the −20 dB/dec ramp — so if you know where ωc sits, you can sketch the whole curve freehand to within a dB or two. Step 3 · Digital Filters

Moving-average zeros: executing one named frequency

Now discrete time: n is an integer sample index. Here ω is digital angular frequency (rad/sample, often written Ω), not the earlier RC model’s rad/s. Given a sample rate Fₛ, ω=2πf/Fₛ; without Fₛ no physical Hz value is assigned. A moving average averages the latest M samples. At ω=2πk/M, provided k is not divisible by M, those M contributions cancel. At 0 and integer multiples of 2π, |H|=1 instead. Predict the first zero, then compare the preset and actual output samples.

|H(ω)| = |sin(Mω/2)| / (M·|sin(ω/2)|); zeros: ω = 2πk/M, integer k not divisible by M; at ω=2πq, |H|=1.

Step 4 · Phase

Phase is character too: lateness wrecks waveforms

The first three steps stared at |H|. Now the other half of the character: phase. Two boxes compete: a pure delay (|H| identically 1 — it cuts no frequency at all — with phase −ωτd growing linearly with frequency) and the RC low-pass (|H| cuts the highs, and its phase −atan(ωτ) is bent). Feed both the same pulse train: the delay’s output is merely late, its shape identical; the RC output has rounded edges and a tail — because its frequency components are delayed by different amounts, the formation they marched in with falls apart. Below are the two phase curves and the RC’s group delay τg(ω) = −dφ/dω, computed by numerically differentiating the phase formula: it varies with frequency, and that variation is exactly why the formation breaks up.

τ = 0.60 s · pure delay: |H| ≡ 1 (0 dB), φ(ω) = −ωτ, group delay constantly τ · RC: group delay falls from 0.600 s at ω=0 to 0.246 s at ω=2 — unequal lateness, so the waveform must deform

Judging by |H| alone breeds two illusions: that a delay “changes nothing” (it changes time itself), and that the RC “only turns the volume down” (it scrambles the waveform). In audio, phase distortion muffles the sound; in images, the equivalent effect blurs edges. Magnitude decides whether each frequency is still present; phase decides whether they still recognize each other when they come back. One more convention: the RC waveform above is the 0–8 s steady segment obtained by pre-integrating the circuit from rest for 8 s, after the transient has essentially died out — not the full switch-on transient from zero. The comparison is about steady-state shape-keeping versus deformation, so the switch-on moment is deliberately excluded.

Step 5 · Different Wiring

High-pass, band-pass, resonance: one mathematics, three appetites

Same R and C — just take the output across the resistor instead, and the low-pass becomes a high-pass on the spot: |H(jω)| = ωτ/√(1+(ωτ)²). Low frequencies are blocked, highs pass, and the −3 dB point sits at the same ωc = 1/τ. Drag τ and watch the cutoff run; compare with step 2’s low-pass Bode plot — the two are mirror images.

RC high-pass τ (ωc = 1/τ) 1.00 s（ωc = 1.00）

τ = 1.00 s · ωc = 1.00 rad/s · at ωc, |H| = 0.7071 and φ = +45.0° (the high-pass leads where the low-pass lags — same magnitude, opposite sign) · at ω = 0.1ωc, |H| = 0.0995 (lows cut to about a tenth)

One more step: put a low-pass and a high-pass back to back so only a middle band of frequencies passes — a band-pass. The standard second-order band-pass H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jQ(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)) peaks at ω₀ with height 1, and the quality factor Q sets how fat the peak is — larger Q, sharper peak. One readout below is a live audit: the half-power bandwidth Δω measured off the curve (the distance between the two points where |H| falls to 1/√2 of the peak) must satisfy Q = ω₀/Δω — whether the measured number matches the slider is for the digits to say. This is a normalized band-pass output. A spring’s displacement/force response has a different numerator, so its peak location and height cannot be copied from this curve. Tuning a radio is band-pass work: the peak aims at the station, and Q decides whether the neighboring station bleeds in. Too small a Q and two stations mush together; too large and the peak is so sharp it shaves the station’s own high frequencies. Even pickiness has to be portioned right.

Step 6 · Finale

Design bench: build a filter to spec

The last step is an exam. The job: in one signal, 50 Hz is the wanted signal and 500 Hz is interference. The toolbox holds three tools; the parameters are yours; press “Verify” and the page computes the attenuation at both frequencies from the chosen filter’s |H| formula, live — how much margin, how many dB short, reported on the spot, no pre-baked verdicts. One spoiler: a first-order RC cannot pass at any setting (its rolloff is too gentle) — a direct consequence of step 2’s −20 dB/dec. The second-order cascade and the moving average each have a way through — and the moving average’s way hides in its zeros: at Fs = 4000 Hz the zeros sit at k·(4000/M) Hz. Two conventions, stated plainly. First, the second-order option is an ideal cascade: the two RC stages are assumed isolated by a buffer amplifier, so |H| is the square of the first-order one; a real passive cascade suffers loading — the second stage drags on the first and the curve changes shape (in hardware you either add the buffer or re-derive the network as a whole). Second, the moving average’s attenuation is computed from the Dirichlet formula with the digital frequency ω = 2πf/Fs at Fs = 4000 Hz; when 500 Hz lands exactly on a zero the attenuation is a numerical zero, displayed capped as “>120 dB”.

The equalizer

The bass / mid / treble knobs on a stereo are a row of band-passes (step 5) with staggered peak frequencies wired in parallel: each governs one slice of the spectrum — a whole equalizer is a frequency-response curve cut into strips.

Image blur

In a photo, high frequencies are edges and detail, low frequencies are broad color fields. A two-dimensional low-pass over the image (average each pixel with its neighbors) is the naive form of Gaussian blur — the same act as step 3’s moving average, with a sheet of pixels instead of a row of samples.

Radio tuning

The air carries hundreds of stations and channels at once; a receiver uses a band-pass to pick out the target frequency and block the rest — step 5’s Q decides how clean the pick is. This bench’s “keep 50, kill 500” is what every receiver does every day.

To wrap up: from medical report to blueprint it is one and the same curve — in step 1 you measured it one frequency at a time, in step 2 you learned the engineer’s way of reading it, steps 3 and 4 showed you the tempers of zeros and of phase, step 5 re-wired the same parts into high-pass and band-pass, and here you have used a spec to build with it. Anyone who can read a frequency response greets every black box with the same first sentence: show me the pass rate and the lateness at every frequency.
