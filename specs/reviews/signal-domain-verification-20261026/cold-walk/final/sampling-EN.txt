Step 0 · Get fooled first

Which way is this wheel actually turning?

The wheel below turns at a steady 2.5 revolutions per second and never changes. The only thing you may touch is the camera’s frame rate: drag the slider and watch it slow down, freeze, and then spin backwards. The wheel never changed — the sampling did. Every camera, and every eye, only peeks at the world once in a while.

Camera frame rate f_s (fps) 14

True speed 2.5 rev/s (constant) · spoke-passing frequency 15 Hz · frame rate 14 fps → apparent speed 0.17 rev/s · forward, same direction

The orange dot on the rim marks the wheel’s true position — it keeps turning honestly, never pausing. The apparent speed is not a guess: fold the spoke-passing frequency with falias = |f − k·fs| and divide by the spoke count, and you get exactly the speed your eyes report. Step 5 checks this formula on the spot.

Step 1 · What sampling is

Can a row of dots remember a wave?

This step uses an ideal continuous sine. Sampling records x[n] = x(tₙ) at tₙ = n/fₛ seconds: n is an integer index, f is cycles per second (Hz), and fₛ counts samples per second. The dots lie on the source. Straight joins or a hold are display interpolations, usually different from the continuous source. More samples can improve these approximations; exact ideal reconstruction also needs a known bandlimit and a suitable reconstruction filter.

Lower fₛ and compare straight joins with a hold: the same source samples give different display curves. These are approximations. Removing the joins does not remove ambiguity: different continuous waves can share every sample. Next, compare two such sines directly.

Step 2 · A fake is born

Same samples, different continuous waves

First, the official name for what is about to happen: aliasing — when the peeks are too sparse, a fast wave gets read as a slow one. Push fs below f and look again: the samples still all sit on the original wave, but by themselves they line up into a different, slower wave. The rose-pink “fake wave” passes through every single sample exactly — from the dots alone there is no way to tell it apart from the original. Its frequency is decided by one formula: fold f by fs, over and over, into the half-room [0, fs/2]. The fold has a direction, too: when the signed folded frequency g is negative (9 Hz sampled at 10 Hz, or 14 Hz at 20 Hz), the fake wave comes out mirrored, its phase flipped — the same event as the wheel turning backwards in step 0, except here it is the waveform that flips.

Signal frequency f (Hz) 9
Sample rate f_s (Hz) 10
Classic slow wave: f=9, f_s=10
Pinned to one phase: f=f_s=10
Folded: f=14, f_s=20
No aliasing: f=3, f_s=25

falias = |f − k·fs|

k is the integer that lands the result in [0, fs/2]. Every readout, spectrum position and audio pitch on this page is computed with this one formula — steps 4 and 5 settle the accounts one by one.

f = 9.0 Hz · f_s = 10.0 Hz · k = 1 · f_alias = |9.0 − 1×10.0| = 1.0 Hz — that is the fake wave

The nastiest preset is “pinned to one phase”: when f is an exact multiple of fs, every peek lands on exactly the same phase, all dots collapse to a single value — no matter how fast the wave, it looks frozen. That is the truth behind the “stopped” wheel in step 0.

Step 3 · The line

The Nyquist line: twice, and not a hair less

This step compares ideal single tones. The Nyquist condition assumes a known bandlimit: if all components satisfy |f| ≤ B and fₛ > 2B, the complete uniform sample sequence determines the signal within that band. Here B=f: sweep fₛ across 2f. Without restricting candidate frequencies, higher aliases can still share every sample. Straight joins are display interpolation; their varying height does not decide exact reconstruction. Pin fₛ=2f and change phase to inspect the boundary ambiguity.

Signal frequency f (Hz) 4
Sample rate f_s (Hz) 10
Sampling phase φ (°) 90
Pin to exactly 2f

f_s = 10.0 Hz > 2f = 8.0 Hz: this tone is inside baseband; uniqueness still assumes a known bandlimit.

At the boundary x[n] = A(−1)ⁿ sinφ. With φ=90° the dots alternate at full amplitude; with φ=0° they vanish. Even nonzero dots determine only A sinφ, not A and φ separately: A=1, φ=30° and A=0.5, φ=90° give identical samples. Nonzero dots are no guarantee of recovery. This page uses strict fₛ>2B with ideal uniform, complete sampling; quantization, noise and finite-record errors are omitted. Basis: TI · sampling and reconstruction →

Notice what aliasing really is: not “the signal got a bit dirty”, but high-frequency information moved bodily into the low frequencies, impersonating them. Once sampling is done, the true peak and the alias peak look identical — no filter afterwards can separate them. Prevention has to happen before sampling.

Step 5 · Aliasing everywhere

It is everywhere in daily life

Three exhibits, one formula. ①Sound: sample a high tone at a low rate, point by point and for real, then play it back — what you hear is not the original, it is the fake wave. ②Images: decimate a fine striped picture pixel by pixel and a moiré grows by itself. ③The wheel: back to step 0, checking the apparent-speed formula on the spot.

Full disclosure: the plot above shows the first 240 sample points of the playback buffer, each computed as the sine value at its true sampling instant t = n/fs — not a low-frequency wave faking it. The pitch you hear is the zero-crossing rate of those very points (the “measured zero-crossings” in the readout is exactly the check a reviewer would run). Silent by default, click to play, volume kept low. Above 10 kHz some people cannot hear the original at all — but its alias falls back into the audible range, where everyone can hear it. Special case: when f is an exact multiple of fs (f = fs, 2fs, …), every sample lands on the same phase (a zero crossing of the sine), so the buffer is all zeros and playback is silence — also true.

Step 6 · Finale

Before you sample, learn to squint

Anti-aliasing suppresses high frequencies before decimation. Both sides use the same source and decimator; the right side first applies a finite box convolution twice. Compare how much moiré weakens and how much detail is lost. A box filter has sidelobes and cannot guarantee zero aliasing; results depend on the pattern and stride. This image example is not an ideal brick-wall low-pass.

Audio also needs anti-alias filtering before analog-to-digital conversion. For a 20 kHz passband and 44.1 kHz sample rate, the Nyquist frequency is 22.05 kHz, leaving a 2.05 kHz transition region for a practical filter to attenuate; it does not cut abruptly at 20 kHz. This illustrates a bandwidth budget, not the historical derivation of 44.1 kHz. Stopband attenuation, hearing limits and hardware performance need separate specifications.

The sampling theorem does not really say “sample as fast as you can”. It says “decide what to give up first, then sample”: you surrender the high-frequency detail precisely so it cannot dress up as low frequency and lie to you.
