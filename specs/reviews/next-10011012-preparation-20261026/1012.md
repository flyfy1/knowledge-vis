## Original contract

Count positive integers no greater than n whose ordinary decimal representation
contains a digit more than once. Count each qualifying integer once, regardless
of how many repeated digits or pairs it contains. The bound is inclusive; zero
is excluded. The only input is n, between 1 and 10^9. The official examples are
20 → 1, 100 → 10, and 1000 → 262.

- Python3: `Solution.numDupDigitsAtMostN(self, n: int) -> int`.
- TypeScript: `numDupDigitsAtMostN(n: number): number`.

No digit list, mask, lower bound, modulus or leading-zero padded representation
is supplied as an original input. The exact official templates are in the wrapper.

## Direct independent examples

`check-examples.py` enumerates integers from 1 through n and compares the length
of each decimal string with its set of characters. This checks the definition
without a digit-state recurrence, permutation formula or complement-count solver.
The largest enumerated bound is 1000; the full qualifying lists are retained.

| n | Count | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, 9, 10 | 0 each | One-digit numbers and 10 have no repeated digit |
| 11, 20 | 1 each | 11 is included at its exact upper boundary |
| 98, 99, 100 | 8, 9, 10 | 99 and 100 each contribute once as the bound increases |
| 101 | 11 | Zero is a real digit after the number begins; the repeated digit here is 1 |
| 110, 111, 112 | 12, 13, 14 | 111 contributes one integer, not three equal-position pairs |
| 1000 | 262 | Multiple repeated zeroes still contribute one integer |

Membership checks also distinguish 102 (all distinct) from 100 and 101. A direct
increment check for bounds 2 through 1000 verifies that the inclusive count grows
by exactly the membership of the newly admitted number. This is an internal
boundary check of the same oracle, not a second independent algorithm or a
full-constraint validation. No one-billion-element enumeration was attempted.
